How each figure is calculated

Total MT
Tonnage shipped, in metric tonnes — kilograms divided by 1,000. Counted once per delivery: Business Central records a shipment several times over (the delivery, a cost adjustment, then the invoice) and repeats the weight on each, so only the entry carrying the item ledger quantity is used. WIN_Total_Qty_in_Kg ÷ 1,000, where Item_Ledger_Entry_Quantity ≠ 0
Revenue
Sales value of the goods shipped, whether or not they have been invoiced yet. A delivery posts an expected value; the invoice reverses it and posts the actual, so both are added together and the figure is right at any stage of the cycle. Sales_Amount_Actual + Sales_Amount_Expected
Cost of sales
What the goods themselves cost — the inventory value released when they left the warehouse. Product cost only: no overheads, freight or delivery charges. It follows the same actual-plus-expected pattern as revenue, so goods shipped but not yet invoiced still carry their cost, and it picks up later cost adjustments once Business Central has posted them. Cost_Amount_Actual + Cost_Amount_Expected
Gross profit
Revenue minus cost of sales. Negative means the goods sold for less than they cost.
GP %
Gross profit as a share of revenue. Worked out from each month's own revenue and cost, never averaged across months, so a small month cannot distort the total.
Price per MT
Average realised selling price per tonne — revenue divided by tonnage. Like GP %, it is worked out from each month's own revenue and tonnage and never averaged across months, so a light month cannot pull the total. A month carrying revenue but no tonnage shows 0.00 rather than an unbounded figure. Revenue ÷ Total MT
What is counted. Sales deliveries, invoices, return receipts and credit memos; returns and credit memos subtract. Internal postings and delivery-charge lines are left out, as the latter earn revenue but carry no tonnage.
Reading the table. Months run left to right, each showing all six figures, with the period total on the right. Every column sorts. A dash means no trade that month.
Product filter. Two levels. Ticking a product group takes all of it; opening the group with the arrow and ticking item descriptions takes only those — NAOH, for instance, covers several specifications at different strengths and prices. Unticking one description inside a whole group leaves the rest of the group selected. The figure beside each line is its revenue under the other filters, so it says what ticking it would actually add.
What they bought. Click a customer name to open the items behind their figures, each shown across the same months and measures. The item lines add up to the customer line, and they follow whichever column you sorted by. GP % and price per MT are worked out from each item's own revenue and tonnage, so they do not add up — the same rule as everywhere else on this tab.
Rounding. Amounts are Singapore dollars, and month cells are abbreviated to thousands (K) or millions (M) to keep the table readable — hover any cell for the exact figure. Totals are worked out at full precision, so a column may differ by a cent from adding up the rounded figures on screen.
Filters. The posting date range, customer, salesperson and product group selections at the top drive everything on this tab — the four headline figures, the table and both charts all move together.
Company. The selector above the tabs decides which company’s ledger every figure on this page is read from, and Group shows both added together. Changing it clears the customer, salesperson and product group selections, since those differ between companies, and keeps the date range. The dashboard opens on April 2026, the period both companies trade in; earlier dates are available and return Chemical Industries only.
The two charts share one scale and one row order, so a product's revenue bar can be read straight across against its cost bar: wherever the lower bar is the longer one, that product sold below cost.

Total MT
Tonnage shipped, in metric tonnes — kilograms divided by 1,000. A point on the line
The total tonnage shipped in that period by that product group — a plain sum, not an average and not a smoothed figure. Changing the grain regroups the same deliveries into larger buckets, so every grain adds up to exactly the same tonnage.
A period with no shipment
Drawn as zero, not as a gap. A group that recorded nothing that period really did record nothing, and a continuous line lets two groups be read against each other over the same span.
A part period
The final point is drawn hollow when its period has not finished — three days into a month, say. It is a bucket still filling, not a fall.
What is counted. The same scope as the other tabs: sales deliveries, invoices, return receipts and credit memos, with internal postings and delivery-charge lines left out. Returns and credit memos subtract, so a period can show a negative figure — a real net return, not an error, and never flipped to positive.
Reading the chart. Height is tonnage in metric tonnes, left to right is time, and each line is one general product posting group. Hover anywhere on the plot for a crosshair and every group's figure at the nearest period — you do not have to hit the line itself.
The scale follows your selection. NAOH is about three quarters of all tonnage, so while it is shown the other six lines sit almost flat. Untick it and the rest rescale. Colour stays with the product group either way, so hiding one never repaints the others.
Filters. The controls in this tile drive the chart and the table together, and the strip under them shows which are narrowing the view. They are independent of the other tabs — a customer filtered here does not narrow anything elsewhere.

How Day, Week and Month are calculated

All three settings show the same postings. The only thing that changes is how many days are added together before a point is drawn. The total is identical whichever you pick — nothing is averaged, estimated or smoothed away.

Day
One point for each date goods were posted, worth the shipment recorded that day. The most detail, but postings arrive in lumps, so the line jumps sharply and the shape is hard to read.
Week
Days are grouped into weeks running Monday to Sunday. All seven days are added together and the point is drawn on the Monday.
Month
Days are grouped by calendar month. Everything posted in a month is added together and the point is drawn on the 1st. This is the default, because it is the setting at which the line reads as a direction of travel rather than a series of jumps.
Which one to use
Month to see the overall direction. Week when a month is too coarse to show movement inside it. Day when you have spotted a jump and want to find the individual posting behind it.
Weeks do not add up to months
A week can begin in one month and end in the next, so its total belongs partly to each. Weeks and months each add up correctly on their own, but you cannot reconcile one week against one month. Only days divide cleanly into both.
Part weeks and part months at the ends of your date range. A week or a month is only shown in full if your date range covers all of it. At either end it can be cut short, and a cut-short point sits lower than a full one simply because it holds fewer days — not because less was shipped.

A common example. You set the range to 1–31 July and read by week. July does not begin on a Monday and does not end on a Sunday, so both ends land mid-week:
• The first point is the week commencing 29 June, but it holds only 1–5 July. 29 and 30 June are excluded by your from date.
• The last point is the week commencing 27 July, but it holds only 27–31 July. 1 and 2 August are excluded by your to date.

Both points hold five days rather than seven, so both sit lower than a full week — and if a large posting happened to fall on one of the excluded days, that point understates the week badly. The same happens by month whenever a range starts or ends part-way through one.

To compare periods fairly, start the range on a Monday and end it on a Sunday when reading by week, and use whole months when reading by month. The figures themselves are always right for the days in range — it is the comparison between points that a cut-short period distorts.

The last column divides the busiest period by a typical one: the lower that figure, the steadier the line and the easier the trend is to read. The totals are there to show that all three settings account for exactly the same amount.

Table view, at the top of this tile, lists the same figures as text at whichever setting is selected — period by period, with totals for each product group and each period — so nothing here can only be read off the chart.

What the chart shows. How Day, Week and Month bucket the data is set out below.

Gross profit
Revenue minus cost of sales, worked out period by period from that period’s own figures. (Sales_Amount_Actual + Sales_Amount_Expected) − (Cost_Amount_Actual + Cost_Amount_Expected)
A point on the line
The gross profit earned in that period by that product group — the period’s revenue less the period’s cost. Changing the grain regroups the same postings into larger buckets, so every grain adds up to exactly the same gross profit.
Below the zero line
A line under the baseline means those goods sold for less than they cost. That is not an error and it is not rare here — whole product groups run negative. The zero line is drawn across the plot for exactly this reason, so loss-making periods are visible at a glance rather than inferred from a small number.
A period with no trade
Drawn as zero, not as a gap. A group that recorded nothing that period really did record nothing, and a continuous line lets two groups be read against each other over the same span.
A part period
The final point is drawn hollow when its period has not finished — three days into a month, say. It is a bucket still filling, not a fall.
What is counted. The same scope as the other tabs: sales deliveries, invoices, return receipts and credit memos, with internal postings and delivery-charge lines left out. Returns and credit memos subtract, so a period can show a negative figure — a real net return, not an error, and never flipped to positive.

The scale follows your selection. One or two groups carry most of the profit, and others sit below zero, so the scale is set by whichever you have on. Untick the largest and the rest rescale. Colour stays with the product group either way, so hiding one never repaints the others.
